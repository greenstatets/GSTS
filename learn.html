Field Notes

What to watch for on your own property.

Real situations from real jobs — the kind of thing a homeowner walks past every day without knowing what they're looking at. If any of these look familiar, it's worth a second look.

Branches touching the roof

When a tree's canopy grows into direct contact with a roofline, it does more than scrape shingles. Constant rubbing wears through roofing material years before it should, and the point of contact holds moisture against the roof long after the rest of it has dried — a slow, quiet path to rot and leaks. It also gives insects and rodents a direct bridge from the canopy onto the structure. If a limb like this fails in a windstorm, it's already resting on the roof it's about to fall onto. Cutting back the clearance is inexpensive compared to what a compromised roof costs to fix.

A cavity in the trunk

An open wound like this on a trunk usually means decay has already worked its way inward, not just across the surface. Bark can look intact everywhere else while a hidden pocket of soft, compromised wood sits right where the tree needs its strength most. The tree isn't necessarily dying — many trees live for decades with a cavity like this — but the structural integrity at that section is genuinely reduced, and it's worth having someone assess how deep it runs and what's still supporting the tree above it.

What's actually inside

This is the same cavity from above, looked at directly. What you're seeing is a hollow running well into the trunk — moss, standing moisture, and open space where solid wood should be. From the ground, a tree like this can still look completely healthy; the canopy leafs out normally because the outer growth rings are still doing their job. The real question isn't whether the tree is alive, it's how much sound wood is actually left to hold the weight above this point in a real wind event.

Caught early

This one is a good example of catching a problem before it becomes expensive. The tree is still young, but its main leaders are already pushing directly against the eave and gutter line. Left alone for a few more growing seasons, this becomes the same roof-contact problem described above — except by then the limbs are thicker, the cuts are bigger, and the tree has already done some real damage to the fascia and gutter. A modest pruning now, while the wood is small, avoids a much larger job later.

Growing into a chimney

A tree canopy that reaches a chimney creates two separate risks. One is mechanical — limbs rubbing brick and mortar over years genuinely wear down the joints. The other is fire: a working chimney throws sparks, and dry foliage sitting directly against the flue is exactly the kind of contact that turns a normal fire into a real problem. Neither risk is dramatic on its own, which is usually why it doesn't get addressed until someone points it out.

Planted too close

Narrow evergreens like this are often planted small, right up against a foundation, without accounting for what they'll look like in ten years. Once a tree is wedged into a gap this tight, it stops being just a landscaping choice and becomes a maintenance problem: moisture gets trapped between the foliage and the siding and stays there, paint and caulking break down faster, and the tree itself is nearly impossible to prune evenly since half of it can't be reached. It's rarely urgent, but it's one of the more common calls we get from owners of ten-plus-year-old homes.

Growth blocking a window

Foundation plantings that outgrow their spot do a few things at once: they block natural light, they hold moisture against the siding right at ground level where it does the most damage, and — worth taking seriously — they can block a window that's meant to be a second way out in an emergency. None of this is dramatic on its own, but it's an easy one to fix and an easy one to forget about, since it happens slowly enough that nobody really notices until the window's gone dark.

What good structure looks like

Worth ending on this one. A well-maintained tree isn't smaller or more restrained than an untouched one — it's just balanced. Weight is distributed evenly through the canopy, there's clearance from the structure and the roofline, and the shape reads as intentional rather than accidental. This is the actual goal of most of the work described above: not removal, not aggressive cutting, just keeping a tree's growth working with the property instead of against it.
